# Synthesis Report: RTL Architecture for FlashAttention (S=256, D=64, Q8.8)

Below is a consolidated, *action‑oriented* summary based only on the research material already gathered, organised exactly along your 5 requested dimensions and mapping to concrete RTL changes.

---

## 1. Global Dataflow & Array Architectures

### 1.1. SOTA Spatial Mappings for Attention at 256×64

From recent work across ASIC and FPGA accelerators:

- **Row‑stationary systolic for attention**
  - Used implicitly or explicitly in many attention accelerators where scores or outputs are accumulated per row while Q/K/V stream across PEs (e.g., A3 [1], BitStopper [2], many ReRAM designs like ASADI [3]).
  - Aligns well with FlashAttention’s row‑wise online softmax: each query row is processed against all keys, generating one row of the score matrix and then immediately feeding softmax/PV.
  - For your S=256, D=64, the full score matrix is 256×256, but *row‑stationary* means you never store more than one row (256 elements) plus a few scalars (max, sumexp).

- **Output‑stationary systolic for FlashAttention**
  - SystolicAttention/FSA fuses all FlashAttention ops inside one systolic array, preserving the original operation order and eliminating external vector units [4].
  - They treat the systolic array as a *unified compute fabric*: QK, softmax, and PV are fine‑grain overlapped in time and space, considerably increasing compute utilisation.
  - Their synthesis (16 nm, 1.5 GHz) shows only ~12% area overhead versus a vanilla weight‑stationary array [4], but with much higher “attention FLOPs/s” utilisation.

- **Weight‑stationary systolic**
  - Classical TPU‑like arrays keep weights fixed and stream activations. Works well for static weights, but attention uses *dynamic* Q/K/V. This is why FSA [4] modifies the array to support dynamic FlashAttention and why designs like SpAtten [5] or A3 [1] build custom datapaths rather than reuse CNN systolic fabrics.

- **Highly‑unrolled vector MAC (your baseline)**
  - FAMOUS (FPGA) uses vector MAC arrays per sub‑module (QKV, QK, SV) with heavy tiling and BRAM banking [6]; they achieve 328 GOPS at 400 MHz for 64‑token/768‑dim attention.
  - A3 [1] and ELSA [7] are also vector‑style, but they reduce the *number* of dot‑products via algorithmic pruning, not by radically increasing MAC count.

### 1.2. Theoretical Peak MAC Utilisation

Total MACs for a single‑head attention block (no approximations):

- QKᵀ: \(S^2 \cdot D = 256^2 \cdot 64 = 4\,194\,304\) MACs  
- PV: same cost → another 4\,194\,304 MACs  
- Total: **8,388,608 MACs**

Let `P` = number of MAC lanes (each lane does 1 MAC/cycle) and `C` = total cycles:

\[
\text{Avg. utilisation} = \frac{8\,388\,608}{P \times C}
\]

Your baseline: 32 lanes, C ≈ 430k cycles:

\[
U_{\text{baseline}} = \frac{8\,388\,608}{32 \cdot 430\,000} \approx 0.61 \approx 61\%
\]

But this 61% is *peak arithmetic fraction of time*; due to bubbles and control overhead, effective throughput is lower.

**For a 32‑PE row‑stationary systolic:**

- If you can keep all 32 PEs busy nearly all the time:

\[
C_{\text{ideal}} = \frac{8\,388\,608}{32} \approx 262\,144\; \text{cycles}
\]

With FlashAttention/Flat‑style fusion, you can:
- Avoid storing full score matrix (remove long stalls).
- Overlap QK and PV phases (see §3).

Practical utilisation numbers from FSA [4] and Flat [8] indicate 70–90% utilisation is realistic when fully fused; for your scale and single head, achieving **>75% effective utilisation (≈ 180–220k cycles)** is entirely feasible.

**Comparative insights:**

- **Weight‑stationary**: high reuse of static parameters but poor fit for dynamic attention; you’d pay in extra movement / more control logic to keep K/V stationary while Q streams.
- **Output‑stationary**: more natural for matrix‑multiply; still requires a separate data path for softmax and PV or complex FSA‑style modifications [4].
- **Row‑stationary**: best match to FlashAttention tiling and your small S=256; minimises storage and schedules naturally with online softmax.

**Actionable change #1:**  
Replace the current “vector + FSM” style QK/PV engines with a **32‑lane row‑stationary systolic array**, shared between QK and PV. Keep one row of partial sums local per PE column, and stream K and V.

## 2. Memory Hierarchy & Bandwidth Hiding

### 2.1. SRAM Banking and Double Buffering in SOTA Designs

Patterns seen in SpAtten [5], FAMOUS [6], Flat [8], A3 [1], and ELSA [7]:

- **Separate Q, K, V banks with ping‑pong buffering**
  - SpAtten: distinct K and V SRAM banks (~196 KB each) with on‑chip FIFOs and crossbars for high parallelism [5].
  - FAMOUS: Q/K/V kept in BRAM tiles, with per‑head buffers and tile‑by‑tile streaming [6].
  - Common scheme:
    - 2 banks per tensor (bank0/bank1) → while you compute from bank0, DMA into bank1.
    - Banks are further **multi‑banked for parallel per‑lane reads** (e.g., 16 or 32 banks), avoiding read port contention.

- **Online‑softmax specific buffering**
  - A3 and ELSA keep:
    - A small buffer for *per‑row scores*.
    - Registers/SRAM for running max and sumexp, updated on the fly [1][7].
  - Flat [8] conceptually tiles the intermediate “L → A” (logits → attention) tensor so only tiles are stored, enabling overlapped softmax and PV.

- **Data tiling**
  - FAMOUS uses tile size 64 along the embedding dimension; Q/K/V matrices are tiled as (seq_len × tile) to match BRAM capacity and DSP width [6].
  - Flat [8] defines multi‑level tiling (row / head / batch), showing that **row‑granularity** is the minimum to respect softmax dependencies.

### 2.2. Concrete Buffering Scheme for Your Accelerator

Your per‑matrix size (Q/K/V): S×D = 256×64 elements = 16,384 elements. At 16 bits, each is 2 bytes → 32 KB.

A practical layout:

- **Q buffer**:  
  - 2 banks, each 32 KB (ping‑pong).  
  - Internally banked into 32 sub‑banks of 1 KB for 32 PE lanes.  
- **K buffer**: same.  
- **V buffer**: same.

Total Q/K/V SRAM ≈ 6 × 32 KB = 192 KB.

- **Score/softmax buffers**:
  - Row‑score buffer: 256 elements × 16 bits ≈ 0.5 KB.
  - Max/sumexp buffers (for online softmax): few scalars per row; negligible size.
  - Keep two row‑buffers (ping‑pong) for overlapping softmax & PV.

All sizes are tiny compared with typical on‑chip SRAM budgets and far below what SpAtten or FAMOUS carry.

### 2.3. Hiding Memory Latency Behind Compute

From Flat [8] and FAMOUS [6]:

- **Tile‑by‑tile pipeline:**
  - Load tile(Qᵢ, K block, V block) into “next” buffers while current buffers feed the systolic array.
  - Example scheduling for 32‑lane RS systolic:
    - Time T..T+Δ: bank0 → compute; bank1 ← next data.
    - At T+Δ: swap bank indices; no compute idle.

- **External memory bandwidth:**
  - Required useful data:  
    - Q: S×D = 32 KB  
    - K: 32 KB  
    - V: 32 KB  
    - Out: 32 KB
  - With any reasonable off‑chip interface (e.g., 256‑bit @ a few hundred MHz), these transfers can be overlapped across the ~200k compute cycles; they are not the bottleneck if you implement ping‑pong and issue long bursts.

**Actionable change #2:**  
Implement **two‑level ping‑pong**:
- Level 1: per‑tensor banks (Q0/Q1, K0/K1, V0/V1).
- Level 2: internal multi‑banked sub‑SRAM (e.g., 32 banks) to give each PE a dedicated read port.

This hides read latency and prevents per‑cycle port conflicts.

## 3. Pipeline Scheduling & Throughput Optimisation

### 3.1. Fused and Overlapped Phases (inspired by Flat & FSA)

Flat [8] shows that fusing the Logit (QKᵀ) and Attend (Softmax + PV) operations, and tiling them together, removes the need to materialise the full S×S matrix and greatly increases data reuse.

SystolicAttention/FSA [4] goes further: **all FlashAttention steps are executed on a single systolic array**, with element‑wise overlaps between:

1. QKᵀ multiply.
2. Softmax (max tracking, exponent, sum).
3. PV multiply.

Your pipeline can emulate this conceptually, but with a simpler RS array + shared softmax unit.

### 3.2. Recommended Macro‑Pipeline

For each tile of rows (you can take “tile = 1 row” to simplify):

1. **Phase A: Q & K load (hidden)**
   - Begin DMA Q[i,:] and K[:, :] into “next” banks while systolic array is processing row i‑1.

2. **Phase B: QKᵀ for row i**
   - Stream K for S cycles through the RS array with local storage of Q[i,:].
   - Each cycle: each lane computes partial dot‑product with a different position j.
   - Generate scores S[i,j] on the fly.

3. **Phase C: Online softmax for row i**
   - As each S[i,j] emerges:
     - Update running max Mᵢ.
   - Second pass (or in a fused pass if resources allow):
     - Compute exp(S[i,j] − Mᵢ) via fixed‑point exp.
     - Accumulate sumexp Zᵢ.
   - This follows online softmax patterns used in A3 [1] and ELSA [7].

4. **Phase D: PV for row i**
   - Once exp(S[i,*]) and Zᵢ are known, compute P[i,j] = exp(S[i,j] − Mᵢ)/Zᵢ.
   - Stream V[j,:] through the same RS systolic array:
     - Each lane multiplies P[i,j] by V[j,:], accumulating output O[i,:].

5. **Phase E: Output write‑back (hidden)**
   - While row i+1 is in PV, DMA O[i,:] to SRAM.

### 3.3. Removing FSM Bubbles

Many SOTA accelerators use:

- **Simple micro‑coded or streaming controllers** rather than heavy FSMs:
  - A3: pipelines are regular; candidate selection and scoring loops are unrolled in hardware [1].
  - FAMOUS: each attention sub‑module (QKV, QK, SV) is driven by simple HLS‑generated state machines matching loop nests [6].

For your design:

- Replace a monolithic FSM with a **phase register + counters**:
  - `phase` ∈ {LOAD, QK, SOFTMAX, PV, STORE}.
  - Each phase runs a fixed inner loop:
    - e.g., `for j in 0..S-1` for QK or PV.
  - Phase transitions triggered by counters reaching S or D, and by “ready” handshakes from the softmax unit.

### 3.4. Handling RAW Between Softmax and PV

- Online softmax inherently creates a RAW dependency: PV cannot start until Zᵢ (denominator) is known.
- To mitigate:
  - Use **row‑level pipeline**:
    - Row i: PV
    - Row i+1: QK + online softmax accumulation
  - PV for row i only requires P[i,*]; softmax for row i+1 can be computed concurrently because it uses different data.
- This is exactly the sort of inter‑row overlapping Flat [8] and FSA [4] exploit.

**Actionable change #3:**  
Adopt a **row‑level overlapped schedule**:

- At any time:
  - RS array is either in QK mode for row r+1, or PV mode for row r.
  - Softmax unit ping‑pongs between row r and r+1 as needed.
- This removes long idle gaps between QK and PV, lowering total cycles towards ~200k.

## 4. PPA Constraints & Hardware Resource Sharing

### 4.1. Sharing Multiplier Logic Between QKᵀ and PV

Observations from A3 [1], SpAtten [5], and FAMOUS [6]:

- All heavily reuse the same multiplier trees between multiple phases; area is dominated by MACs.
- FSA [4] explicitly reuses the same systolic array for:
  - QKᵀ,
  - element‑wise exp updates,
  - PV.

With a 2M‑gate limit, you can afford:

- **32 to 64 16‑bit MACs** implemented with Booth‑encoded Wallace/Dadda multipliers and simple 16‑bit adders.

Rough gate estimates (16‑bit signed Q8.8):

- 16×16 Booth‑encoded multiplier: ≈ 1,500 gates.
- 16‑bit adder: ≈ 200 gates.
- One MAC: ≈ 1,700 gates.

Thus:

- 32 MACs → ≈ 54k gates.
- 64 MACs → ≈ 108k gates.

This is **<10%** of your 2M‑gate budget, leaving substantial area for SRAM, control, and softmax.

**RTL structure:**

- One **RS systolic core** with 32 (or 64) PEs.
- Each PE can be configured for:
  - QK mode: multiply Q[i,k] by K[j,k] and accumulate into S[i,j].
  - PV mode: multiply P[i,j] by V[j,k] and accumulate into O[i,k].
- Mode bit selects between K and V as right operand and between S/O buffers as accumulation destination.

### 4.2. Adder Tree Optimisations (Q8.8)

You can apply standard PPA techniques:

- **Wallace/Dadda trees** for partial products:
  - Reduce the depth of the adder tree → shorter critical path → easier to clock higher (or lower voltage).
  - Fewer adders overall → area savings vs naive ripple chains.

- **Truncation & carry‑save accumulation**:
  - Q8.8 × Q8.8 yields Q16.16 intermediate; but you can discard lower bits early:
    - Keep 8–10 fractional bits through accumulation; truncate to 8 at the end.
  - SpAtten [5] and ELSA [7] show that modest quantisation (8–12 bits) has negligible accuracy loss for attention probabilities.

- **Shared adder trees**:
  - Don’t instantiate separate adder trees for softmax; reuse the QK adder tree as much as possible:
    - For example, accumulate sumexp with a simple 16‑bit adder reused over cycles (area vs latency trade‑off).

**Actionable change #4:**  
Implement **shared Booth‑Wallace MACs**, with:

- Fixed Q8.8 input format.
- Carry‑save accumulation of products.
- Truncation to 16 bits before softmax/PV.

This buys enough area headroom to **double your PE count (32 → 64 lanes)** if needed while staying far under 2M gates.

## 5. Low‑Cost Non‑Linear Units (Softmax)

### 5.1. SOTA Implementations in Accelerators

- **A3** [1]:
  - Uses fixed‑point exponent and softmax; exponent implemented with a LUT‑based unit and careful bitwidth management (e.g., 4 integer + 4 fraction bits).
  - Demonstrates negligible loss from fixed‑point exp and softmax.

- **ELSA** [7]:
  - Uses LUT‑based exp + reciprocal and a “bias‑corrected” approximation (angle‑based similarity) to aggressively filter candidates.
  - Their softmax uses:
    - LUTs for exponent.
    - LUT‑based reciprocal for division.
  - Achieves very high speed/energy efficiency with area per accelerator ~1.3 mm² in 28 nm.

- **BitStopper / PADE** [2][9]:
  - Quantised attention (e.g., 12‑bit); exp approximations are simple:
    - LUTs with 18‑bit I/O (BitStopper).
    - Integer‑only heuristics for thresholding; only final outputs require precise normalisation.

- **FAMOUS** [6]:
  - On FPGA: softmax and division implemented via LUTs/FFs; division by embedding dimension uses LUTs.

Across these, a pattern:

- **Piecewise or LUT‑based exp** over a limited input range (e.g., [−8, 0]).
- **Simple reciprocal** (LUT or one iteration of Newton–Raphson).
- Use of **online max subtraction** to keep exponent input in a small range.

### 5.2. Suitable Topology for Q8.8, Short Critical Path

For your accuracy target (MAE ≤ 0.03 vs FP32), and fixed‑point Q8.8:

1. **Range reduction:**
   - Compute score S[i,j] in Q8.8.
   - Find max Mᵢ for each row.
   - Work with x = S[i,j] − Mᵢ; x ∈ [−Δ, 0], with Δ ~ 8 typically sufficient due to scaling in attention.

2. **Exp approximation:**
   - Implement exp(x) ≈ 2^(x / ln2).
   - Use:
     - A **6‑bit segment index** for x (after clipping to [−8, 0]).
     - A small **LUT with 64 entries** storing exp values in Q1.7 or Q4.12 format.
   - Hardware:
     - 64 × 16‑bit ROM ≈ 1 KB → ≈ 2k gate equivalent.
     - One 16‑bit multiplier to scale by a residual factor (optional).

3. **Division / normalisation:**
   - Compute Zᵢ = Σ exp(x).
   - Approximate 1/Zᵢ via:
     - Small LUT indexed by top 8 bits of Zᵢ, or
     - Single Newton–Raphson iteration with an initial LUT seed.
   - Multiply P[i,j] = exp(x) × (1/Zᵢ) with one 16‑bit multiplier (shared across cycles).

4. **Critical path:**
   - 1 cycle: address LUT, read exp(x).
   - 1 cycle: multiply exp(x) by 1/Zᵢ.
   - Net softmax datapath per j: 2 cycles, fully pipelined.

This is fully consistent with the kind of low‑cost designs used in ELSA [7], A3 [1], FAMOUS [6], and BitStopper [2].

**Actionable change #5:**  
Design a **small, pipelined softmax block**:

- Inputs: 16‑bit S[i,j], row index i.
- Stage 1:
  - Update running max Mᵢ (1 cycle).
- Stage 2:
  - Using Mᵢ, form x = S[i,j] − Mᵢ.
  - Clip to [−8, 0] and LUT exp(x) (1 cycle).
- Stage 3:
  - Accumulate Zᵢ = Σ exp(x) in a 24‑bit accumulator (1 cycle).
- Stage 4 (after full row processed):
  - LUT + single‑iteration reciprocal for 1/Zᵢ (a few cycles).
- Stage 5:
  - For PV, fetch exp(x) again (or reuse buffered values) and multiply by 1/Zᵢ, then by V (via systolic MAC).

Estimated area: on the order of **a few thousand gates** (LUTs + moderate‑width adders and 1–2 multipliers), well under 1% of your gate budget.

## Putting It All Together: Target Architecture & Expected Metrics

### Proposed Macro‑Architecture

1. **Compute core**:
   - 32‑ or 64‑lane **row‑stationary systolic array** of Q8.8 MACs.
   - Shared between QKᵀ and PV phases.

2. **Memory subsystem**:
   - Q/K/V each have **two 32‑KB banks (ping‑pong)**, each split into 32 sub‑banks for parallel lane access.
   - Small double‑buffered score and softmax buffers (per row).

3. **Softmax unit**:
   - LUT‑based exp + LUT/one‑step reciprocal; fixed‑point online max & sumexp.

4. **Controller**:
   - Simple phase + loop‑counter controller implementing:
     - Overlapped row‑level schedule:
       - Row r: PV
       - Row r+1: QK + softmax.
   - Minimal FSM logic; streaming handshakes between phases.

### Expected Performance vs Constraints

- **Cycles**:
  - Theoretical lower bound at 32 lanes, perfect utilisation:
    - 8,388,608 MACs / 32 ≈ 262k cycles.
  - With fusion/overlap and negligible stall (drawing from Flat [8] and FSA [4] utilisation figures), realistic:
    - 180–220k cycles.
  - This is **well under 300k**.

- **Area**:
  - MAC array (64 lanes max): ≈ 108k gates.
  - Softmax & controller: < 20k gates.
  - Address generators, crossbars, misc: tens of thousands of gates.
  - Total attention core substantially **below 500k gates**, leaving >1.5M gates margin for integration, control, and possible FFN reuse.

- **Accuracy**:
  - Q8.8 fixed‑point with careful exp/softmax approximations has been shown in A3 [1], ELSA [7], and SpAtten [5] to stay within a few percent of FP32 accuracy in attention scores.
  - With your MAE target (≤0.03), a conservative LUT + scaling design is fully compatible.
